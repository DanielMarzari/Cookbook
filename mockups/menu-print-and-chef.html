The menu, and what the chef sees

Two sides of the same evening. The guest gets a printed card — choices where there are choices, dietary marks where they matter, in whatever aesthetic suits the night. You get the views that tell you whether the menu actually works before anyone sits down.

Not a translation of the other three. Picking this aesthetic picks a course grammar too — antipasto, primo, secondo, contorno, dolce is a different shape of meal, not the same meal in Italian. Six dishes here, all from your own Italian and Roman recipes, and the choice points are gone because an Italian menu does not offer them.

B · What the chef sees

Six more views, all answering a question the card cannot. Lanes from the last mockup catches repetition; these catch the other ways an evening goes wrong.

03

Contention

Not "when is each dish made" but "what does it need, and does something else need it at the same moment." Home kitchens have one oven and four burners, and that — rather than flavour — is what actually breaks a menu.

Catches: the oven is asked for three different temperatures in two hours, and the last two overlap. The fix is a menu decision, not a timing one — move the alfajores to the day before, or the braciole.

04

Bridges

What carries one course into the next. A menu that reads as a menu rather than a sequence of dinners has a thread running through it — a repeated note, a technique echo, a colour. A thin link is a jolt at the table.

Catches: one dead link. Dates to braciole shares no ingredient, no technique and no temperature — it is where the meal changes subject. Either bridge it or make the break deliberate and put a pause there.

05

The whole meal, at once

Every course summed into one shape. Not for fixing a single plate — for answering "what kind of dinner is this," and noticing when the answer is narrower than you meant it to be.

Catches: a menu that is basically rich and sweet. Acid and bitter are almost at the centre, and those are the two that make a long dinner survivable. One sharp course anywhere in the back half changes the shape.

06

Same dish twice

Runs the menu against the canon families. Two courses out of one family are the same dish in different clothes, however far apart they look on the card — and this is the only check here that needs the cookbook to already know something.

Collision · dough with filling
Vori vori and braciole are both a wrapped or bound starch around a savoury filling, simmered in their own liquid. Different continents, same move — and they sit next to each other.
Clear · fried sweet
Globi is the only fried thing in the menu, and the only dough-in-hot-fat. Nothing shadows it.
Clear · pounded raw
Epityrum is alone in the dip family. The za'atar beside it is a dry blend, not a second pounded relish.
Thin · nothing raw, nothing cold after the first course
Six of seven courses are cooked and hot. The family spread is narrow even where no two dishes collide outright.

Catches: braciole and vori vori are the same idea — which the lanes view half-found by spotting beef twice, and this one names.

Where each of these gets its data. Diet marks come off the recipe ingredients, so the card and view 01 cannot disagree. Prep pooling and contention come from the instruction steps already stored per recipe. Bridges and the balance wheel read note_profiles and the ingredient_cooccur corpus behind the dish score. Same dish twice reads the canon families. The chef views are checks on one authored thing — the list of recipes you picked — so none of them can contradict the card.
