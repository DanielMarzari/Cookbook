Six ways to plan a tasting menu

A menu is not a list of good dishes. It is a sequence, and the sequence can be wrong even when every dish in it is right. Each view below is the same seven courses — built from recipes already in the cookbook — drawn so a different kind of mistake becomes visible.

The menu under test

Seven courses, all from your own recipes. It has three real problems, and none of them are visible when you write it out as a list — which is how menus get written.

01

The arc

Richness plotted across the meal. A tasting menu is supposed to climb, peak once, and come down — so the shape itself is the thing you are designing, and a bad menu has a bad silhouette.

Catches: the menu never comes down. Courses IV to VI sit in the top third with nothing light between them, so the braciole reads as one more heavy plate rather than the peak.

03

The card

What the table actually reads. Not a planning tool — the output — but worth designing alongside the others, because a menu that plans well and reads badly is still a menu you have to hand someone.

Catches: nothing — and that is the point. Read as a card the menu is handsome, which is exactly why the other five views exist.

04

The kitchen

The same seven courses against when the work actually happens. A menu is also a labour plan, and the constraint that sinks a dinner at home is almost never flavour — it is four things wanting the stove at once.

Catches: three courses finish at the stove during service, and two of them are fried or dumpling work that cannot wait. Swap one for something that reheats and the evening stops being a shift.

06

The spine

The arc turned on its side so it reads as a list. Loses the curve, keeps the proportion, and it is the only one of these six that works on a phone in a kitchen — which is where you would actually be holding it.

IEpityrum · cold, sharp
IIStuffed dates · room
IIIHoney mushrooms · hot
IVVori vori · hot, beef
VBraciole · hot, beef
VIGlobi · hot, fried
VIIAlfajores · cold

Catches: the same plateau as the arc, without the drawing. The step from IV to V is the only real climb in the menu, and it arrives after four courses of steady build rather than off a dip.

What the real thing would know. None of the above is hand-entered. Richness, acid and the ingredient grid come from the flavour data already loaded — note_profiles and the ingredient_cooccur corpus behind the dish score. Seasonality comes from the seasonal tables, so a menu can be checked against the month you are cooking it in. Canon families give the one check none of these views show: two courses out of the same family are the same dish twice, however different they look on the card.
